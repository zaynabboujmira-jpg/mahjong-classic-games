The opening is where many beginners feel lost: there are thousands of named lines, and stronger players seem to know them all. The good news is that you do not need them. A handful of principles will give you a healthy position in almost every game, against people and against the computer. Here are the ten that matter most.

## 1. Take the centre with a pawn

The four central squares (e4, d4, e5 and d5) are the most important on the board. Pieces placed near them control more squares and can reach either side quickly. Start with **1.e4** or **1.d4** as White. As Black, answer with a centre pawn of your own: 1...e5 or 1...c5 against e4, and 1...d5 or 1...Nf6 against d4.

## 2. Develop knights before bishops

Knights have fewer good squares than bishops, and their best squares (f3 and c3 for White, f6 and c6 for Black) are clear from the start. Bringing them out first also keeps your options open for where the bishops belong. "Knights before bishops" is not a law, but it is a reliable habit.

## 3. Move each piece once

Every move in the opening should bring a new piece into play. Moving the same piece twice, unless you are capturing or escaping a threat, hands your opponent a free move. A useful self-check: after ten moves, have your knights and bishops all moved?

## 4. Do not bring the queen out early

The queen is powerful, but in the opening it is a target. If it comes out on move two or three, your opponent can attack it with developing moves (a knight here, a bishop there) and you have to keep moving it. Develop your minor pieces first, then find the queen a safe square, usually e2, d2 or c2 (e7, d7 or c7 for Black).

## 5. Castle early

Castling moves your king to safety and brings a rook toward the centre in one move. Aim to castle within the first ten moves, usually on the kingside, which needs only the knight and bishop to move. A king left on e1 while the centre opens is the most common way beginner games end early.

## 6. Connect your rooks

After castling and moving your minor pieces and queen, your two rooks should see each other along the back rank. Connected rooks protect each other and are ready to move to open files. When your rooks are connected, the opening is finished.

## 7. Do not grab pawns at any cost

Taking a pawn is good when it is free. Taking one that costs you three moves of development, or pulls your queen far away, usually is not. Ask what your opponent gets in return: open lines, faster development or an attack on your king.

## 8. Avoid weakening pawn moves

Moving the pawns in front of your castled king (f, g and h pawns for a kingside castle) creates holes your opponent can use. Early on, move only the pawns you need for the centre and for development, typically the e, d and sometimes c pawns.

## 9. Know the beginner traps

Two quick attacks catch many beginners:

- **The four-move checkmate.** White plays e4, Bc4 and Qh5 (or Qf3) and checkmates on f7. Protect e5 with ...Nc6, answer Qh5 with ...g6 and Qf3 with ...Nf6, and remember that f7 is weak until you castle.
- **Hanging pieces.** A piece that is attacked and not defended can simply be taken. Before every move, check that you are not leaving one behind.

## 10. Check threats before every move

This is the most important rule in the whole game. Before you move, ask: **what did my opponent's last move threaten?** Captures, checks and attacks on undefended pieces come first. Most beginner games are not lost in the opening; they are lost when a threat goes unnoticed.

## Common beginner mistakes, and the fix

| Mistake | Why it hurts | Better habit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pushing edge pawns (a- and h-pawns) early | Gains no space in the centre and develops nothing | Move a centre pawn or a knight instead |
| Moving the f-pawn before castling | Opens the diagonal to your king | Keep f2 (or f7) at home until the king is safe |
| Developing a bishop behind its own pawns | The bishop has no open diagonal | Move the e- or d-pawn first, then the bishop |
| Trading a bishop for a knight for no reason | Gives away the bishop pair cheaply | Trade only when it wins time, material or damages the pawns |

When you review a game, look for these four first. Fixing them is worth more than learning any named opening.

## A simple opening plan for White

1. e4
2. Nf3
3. Bc4 or Bb5
4. Nc3 or d3
5. Castle kingside
6. Connect your rooks and look for a plan in the centre.

As Black against 1.e4, a similar plan works: ...e5, ...Nc6, ...Nf6, ...Bc5 or ...Be7, then castle.

## Practise against the computer

The chess game on this site has three computer levels. **Beginner** looks one move ahead and makes mistakes you can punish, which makes it perfect for practising these principles. **Club** plays solid moves and will punish a king left in the centre. **Tactician** looks several moves ahead and finds loose pieces quickly. Play a few games at each level as White and Black, and use undo to replay any opening that went wrong.
