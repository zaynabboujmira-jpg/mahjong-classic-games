Chess and checkers look like close relatives. They use the same chequered board, two armies face each other from opposite sides, and both reward thinking ahead. But the games play very differently. This guide compares them side by side: the setup, the rules, the goal, the difficulty and what each teaches you.

## At a glance

| | Chess | Checkers |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Board | 8×8, all 64 squares | 8×8, only the 32 dark squares |
| Pieces per player | 16, of six kinds | 12, all the same |
| How pieces move | Each kind moves its own way | Diagonally forward, one square |
| Captures | By landing on a piece; never forced | By jumping over a piece; forced |
| Promotion | A pawn on the last rank becomes any piece | A man on the last row becomes a king |
| Goal | Checkmate the king | Capture or block every enemy piece |
| Who moves first | White | The darker colour (Red on this site) |
| Draws | Common at high level | Very common at high level |
| Solved? | No | Yes: perfect play is a draw |

## The board and the pieces

Both games use an **8×8 board**, so one set can serve for both. In chess, all 64 squares are used and the board is turned so each player has a light square in the bottom-right corner. In checkers, only the **dark squares** are used, and each player has a dark square in the bottom-left corner.

Chess gives each player **16 pieces of six kinds**: king, queen, two rooks, two bishops, two knights and eight pawns. Checkers gives each player **12 identical pieces**, called men. [Chess Pieces: Names, Moves and Values](/blog/chess-pieces-names-and-moves/) covers each chess piece; [How to Play Checkers](/blog/how-to-play-checkers-rules/) covers the setup and moves of checkers.

## How the pieces move and capture

In checkers, every man moves the same way: **one square diagonally forward**. It captures by **jumping** over an enemy piece next to it into the empty square beyond, and it can chain several jumps in one turn. Captures are **compulsory**: if you can jump, you must.

In chess, each kind of piece has its own move, from the king's single step to the queen's long lines and the knight's L-shaped leap. A piece captures by **moving onto** an enemy piece's square. Captures are **never forced**, which gives each player far more choice on every move.

## The goal of each game

This is the biggest difference. In **checkers**, pieces are the goal: you win by **capturing all of your opponent's pieces or leaving them with no legal move**. Every exchange changes the material count directly.

In **chess**, the goal is the **king**. You win by **checkmate**: attacking the king so that it cannot escape. You can be far behind in material and still win with a mating attack, which is why chess has so many sacrifices.

## Kings and promotion

Both games reward reaching the far side. A checkers man on the last row is **crowned king** and can then move and jump diagonally **backwards as well as forwards**. A chess pawn on the last rank **promotes**, usually to a queen, the strongest piece on the board. In both games the race to promote often decides the endgame.

## Which is harder?

By most measures, **chess is more complex**. Checkers has about 5 × 10²⁰ possible positions; chess has around 10⁴⁴. In **2007**, researchers led by Jonathan Schaeffer proved that checkers is a **draw with perfect play**, using the program Chinook after many years of computation. Chess is nowhere near being solved.

That does not make checkers easy. Because jumps are forced, a strong player can set up traps many moves deep, offering one piece to force a jump that opens a double or triple capture in return. [Checkers Strategy: Forced Jumps and Endgame Tactics](/blog/checkers-forced-jumps-and-endgame-tactics/) shows how.

## What each game teaches

- **Checkers** teaches calculation and patience: counting forced sequences, keeping your back row safe and trading when you are ahead.
- **Chess** teaches planning and variety: developing several kinds of pieces, controlling the centre, protecting the king and using tactics like forks and pins. [Chess Strategies for Beginners](/blog/chess-strategies-for-beginners/) is a good first read.

## Which should you learn first?

If you are new to board games or teaching a young child, **start with checkers**. The rules fit in a minute, and every game has a clear finish. Move on to **chess** when you want more variety and depth. Many players enjoy both: checkers for a quick, sharp game, and chess for a longer battle of plans.

Skills carry over in both directions. Checkers trains you to look for forced sequences and to count exchanges exactly, which helps with chess tactics. Chess trains you to think about the whole board and long-term plans, which helps you see where a checkers endgame is heading.
