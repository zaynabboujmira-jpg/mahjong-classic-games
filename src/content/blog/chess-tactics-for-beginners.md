Ask a strong player how to improve at chess and the answer is nearly always the same: study tactics. Most games between beginners are not decided by deep plans but by a single fork, pin or missed checkmate. This guide explains the tactics you will meet most often, the checkmate patterns worth knowing by heart and a routine for spotting them in your own games.

## What is a chess tactic?

A tactic is a **short sequence of forcing moves** (checks, captures and threats) that wins material or delivers checkmate. Because the moves are forcing, your opponent has few choices, so you can calculate the whole sequence. Most tactics use one idea: **attacking two things at once**, so your opponent cannot save both.

## The fork

A **fork** is one piece attacking two or more enemy pieces at the same time. Your opponent can only move one of them, so you win the other.

Every piece can fork, but the **knight** is the most dangerous, because its L-shaped move is easy to miss. A classic example is a knight landing on c7, attacking the king on e8 and the rook on a8 at once. The king must move, and the rook is lost. Pawns fork too: a pawn pushed between two pieces attacks both.

**Look for:** two enemy pieces that are a knight's move apart from the same square, especially the king and an undefended piece.

## The pin

A **pin** attacks a piece that cannot move without exposing a more valuable piece behind it on the same line. Only bishops, rooks and queens can pin, because they move along lines.

- An **absolute pin** is against the king: the pinned piece is not allowed to move at all, because that would put its own king in check.
- A **relative pin** is against another valuable piece, such as the queen. Moving is legal, but costly.

For example, after 1.d4 d5 2.c4 e6 3.Nc3 Nf6 4.Bg5, White's bishop pins the knight on f6 to Black's queen on d8. Once a piece is pinned, **attack it again**: a pinned piece is a poor defender and an easy target.

## The skewer

A **skewer** is a pin in reverse. You attack a valuable piece that has a less valuable piece behind it on the same line. The valuable piece must move, and you capture the one behind.

Skewers are common in endgames: a rook gives check from the side, the king steps away, and the rook takes the queen or rook standing on the same rank behind it.

## Discovered attacks and discovered checks

A **discovered attack** happens when you move one piece out of the way and reveal an attack by another piece behind it. The piece that moves can make a threat of its own, so your opponent faces two threats at once.

If the revealed attack is on the king, it is a **discovered check**, one of the strongest tactics in chess: the moving piece can go almost anywhere, even capture the queen, because your opponent must deal with the check first. If both pieces give check at once, it is a **double check**, and the only answer is to move the king.

## Removing the defender

Sometimes a piece is safe only because another piece defends it. **Capture or chase away the defender**, and the piece it was guarding falls. Related ideas:

- **Deflection:** force a defender to leave the square or line it is guarding.
- **Overloading:** a single piece defends two things; attack both and it cannot hold them.
- **Decoy:** sacrifice a piece to lure an enemy piece, often the king, onto a square where a tactic works.

## Checkmate patterns to know

Many checkmates repeat the same shapes. Learn these and you will spot them quickly:

| Pattern | The idea |
| --- | --- |
| Back-rank mate | A rook or queen mates a king trapped on its back rank by its own pawns |
| Smothered mate | A knight mates a king boxed in by its own pieces, such as a king on h8 with a rook on g8 and pawns on g7 and h7, mated by a knight on f7 |
| Queen and king mate | The queen covers the king's escape squares while your king protects the queen next to it |
| Two-rook ladder | Two rooks take turns checking, pushing the king to the edge, then mate |
| Scholar's mate | 1.e4 e5 2.Bc4 Nc6 3.Qh5 Nf6?? 4.Qxf7#: queen and bishop hit f7 |

The **back-rank mate** is the one that catches beginners most often. Moving one pawn in front of a castled king (often h3 or h6) gives the king an escape square and prevents it.

## How to spot tactics in your games

Before every move, check three things for both sides: **checks, captures and threats**. Ask:

1. Are any pieces undefended or defended only once?
2. Are the enemy king and queen on the same line or a knight's move apart from the same square?
3. Is any piece doing two jobs at once?

If the answer is yes, there may be a tactic, for you or against you. [Chess Strategies for Beginners](/blog/chess-strategies-for-beginners/) covers the habits that keep your own pieces safe.
