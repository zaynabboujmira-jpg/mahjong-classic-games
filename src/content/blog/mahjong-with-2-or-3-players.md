Mahjong is a game for four, but you will not always have four players. The good news is that Mahjong adapts well to three or even two. The changes are house rules rather than one official standard, so this guide explains the common ways to do it and why each change helps. If you are new to the game, start with [How to Play Mahjong: Rules for Beginners](/blog/how-to-play-mahjong-beginners/).

## Why four is the standard

The four-player game is balanced around four seats, one for each wind. The wall of 136 or 144 tiles is built in four sides, each player has a turn in every round, and calling discards (pung, kong, chow) depends on there being three other players to discard. Take a player away and two things change: the wall lasts longer, and there are fewer discards to claim. Most three- and two-player rules fix exactly those two things.

## Mahjong for 3 players

These are the most common adjustments, and you can mix them as you like:

1. **Leave one seat empty.** Usually the North seat is removed and the North wind tiles become bonus tiles or are simply kept as an honour pung. The other three winds rotate as normal.
2. **Remove some tiles.** The most popular choices are to remove the **characters 2 to 8**, keeping the 1s and 9s (this is the Japanese three-player game, known as sanma), or to remove **one whole suit**. Either way the wall is shorter and hands finish sooner.
3. **No chow.** With only two opponents, a chow from the left-hand player would be very easy to make. Most three-player games allow only pung and kong calls, and a discard for a win.
4. **Deal and play as normal.** Each player starts with 13 tiles (the dealer with 14, and discards first), and turns go counterclockwise.

Scoring follows your usual rules, but because the tiles are less varied, all one suit and all pungs become much more common. Some groups raise the minimum score for a win, for example to 3 or more faan in Hong Kong rules, to keep hands interesting.

## Building the wall for 3

With three players, build the wall on **three sides** instead of four, or build all four sides and let the empty seat's wall be drawn from like any other. Roll the dice to decide where the wall is broken, deal 13 tiles to each player, and keep a dead wall for kong replacements as usual. If you removed the characters 2 to 8, there are 108 tiles (plus any flowers), so each side of a three-sided wall is 18 stacks of two.

Rotate the dealer as normal: the deal passes counterclockwise after the dealer loses, and a round ends once each of the three players has dealt. Many groups play a **two-round game**, East then South, to keep the evening short.

## Mahjong for 2 players

Two-player Mahjong works best as a fast head-to-head game:

- **Remove one or two suits.** With a full set, two players can draw for a long time without much interaction. Removing the characters (or both characters and dots) keeps hands quick.
- **No chow, and often no flowers.** Pung, kong and winning on a discard are allowed. Flowers and seasons can stay as bonus tiles, but many players remove them.
- **Build a smaller wall.** Two sides of the wall in front of each player are enough.
- **Keep the scoring simple.** Many players count the winning hand with the normal table, or just one point per win, and play an agreed number of hands.

Because your opponent discards only every second tile, you will claim fewer discards. Plan for a **concealed hand** that wins on a self-drawn tile, and keep pairs that could become pungs.

## What changes in strategy

| | 4 players | 3 players | 2 players |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Discards you can claim | From 3 opponents | From 2 | From 1 |
| Chow allowed | Yes (from the left) | Usually no | Usually no |
| Typical tiles | 136 or 144 | About 108 | About 100 or fewer |
| Best plans | Any | One suit, pungs | Concealed, pungs |

With fewer players, **defence matters less** and **speed matters more**. Each opponent's discards also tell you more, because there are fewer of them to read.

## American Mahjong with fewer players

American Mahjong uses jokers, the Charleston and a card of hands that changes every year. Groups often play it with three, skipping the across pass in the Charleston, and with two by dealing as for four and leaving two hands unused. These are house rules too; agree on them first. [American vs Hong Kong Mahjong](/blog/american-vs-hong-kong-mahjong-rules/) explains the differences between the two styles.

## Playing alone: four seats, one player

If you want the full four-player game but have nobody to play with, play against the computer. The [Hong Kong Mahjong game on this site](/mahjong/hong-kong/) seats you against three computer players, with flowers and a 3-faan minimum, and you can also choose [Chinese Classic](/mahjong/chinese/) or [American](/mahjong/american/) rules. It is the quickest way to practise calling, defence and scoring before your next game with friends.
