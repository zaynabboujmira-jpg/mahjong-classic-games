Checkers looks simple: twelve pieces each, diagonal moves, jumps to capture. The rule that makes it deep is that **jumps are compulsory**. Your opponent must take a piece when one is offered, which means you can choose their move for them. Good checkers players use this constantly. This guide covers the forced-jump tactics that win material, the habits that keep your position solid, and the endgame technique that turns an extra piece into a win.

## The rules that matter for strategy

In American checkers (also called English draughts) on an 8×8 board:

- Pieces move one square diagonally forward.
- A capture jumps over an adjacent enemy piece to the empty square behind it.
- **Captures are mandatory**, and a piece must keep jumping if it can.
- A piece reaching the far row becomes a king and can move and capture backwards too.

The game on this site follows these rules: compulsory jumps, multi-jump chains, and kings that move both ways.

## 1. Use forced jumps to set up multi-jumps

The basic trick: give up one piece so that your opponent's capturing piece lands on a square from which you can jump two or three pieces in reply. Before every move, look for positions where an enemy piece, after taking yours, would sit between two of their own pieces with empty squares behind them. These are the setups that win material.

## 2. Look for the "two for one" shot

The most common forced-jump combination trades one of your pieces for two of theirs. You place a piece where it must be taken; the capturing piece moves into a spot where your piece can now jump it and continue onto a second enemy piece. If you see your opponent's pieces lined up diagonally with gaps behind them, look for this shot.

## 3. Check what you are forced to take

The forced-jump rule works against you too. Before every move, ask: **if my opponent offers a piece next turn, where will my capturing piece land?** Many games are lost by a single move that lets the opponent force a capture into a trap.

## 4. Keep your back row

Your back row stops your opponent from crowning. Keeping two pieces there, especially on the squares that guard your double corner, makes it very hard for them to make a king. Move back-row pieces only when you have to, and usually late in the middle game.

## 5. Control the centre

Pieces in the middle of the board have the most options and support each other. Advance in connected groups, with pieces backing each other up diagonally, so that a capture of one can be answered by a recapture. A lone piece far up the board is easy to trap.

## 6. Avoid the edges early

Edge pieces can only move in one direction and cannot be jumped from the side, which makes them safe but slow. Too many pieces on the edges leave you with few moves, and running out of good moves in checkers often means being forced into a bad one.

## 7. Trade when ahead, avoid trades when behind

With an extra piece, every one-for-one exchange makes your advantage bigger: eight against seven is close, three against two is usually winning. When you are behind, keep pieces on the board and look for forced-jump shots to win material back.

## 8. Race to crown when the path is clear

A king is worth much more than an ordinary piece, especially in the endgame, because it can move backwards. If one of your pieces has a clear path to the far row and your opponent cannot stop it, crowning it is often the strongest plan.

## The endgame

### Two kings against one

This is always a win if done correctly. The lone king will run to a double corner, where it can shuttle between two squares. Use your two kings to take away those squares one at a time, forcing it out to a single corner or the edge, where it can be trapped.

### Three kings against two

Also a win, but slower. Use your extra king to force a trade: offer a king so that their capture lets you jump back, leaving you two kings against one. Then use the technique above.

### One king against one king

Usually a draw, because the defending king can shuttle in the double corner. If you are the one defending, head for a double corner as soon as the material runs low.

## A practice plan

1. Play the computer on Easy and look for one forced-jump shot each game.
2. Move up to Medium and focus on keeping your back row and trading when ahead.
3. On Hard, practise endgames: when you get two kings against one, finish the win cleanly.

Use undo freely. Going back to the move before a lost combination is the fastest way to learn to see it coming.
