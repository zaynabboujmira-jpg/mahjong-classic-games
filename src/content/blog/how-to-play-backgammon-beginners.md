Backgammon is one of the oldest board games still played, and one of the best mixes of luck and skill ever designed. The dice decide what you can do; you decide what to do with it. The rules fit on a page, and this guide covers all of them, plus a simple plan for your first games.

## The board

The board has **24 narrow triangles called points**, in four quarters of six. Each player has a **home board** (where checkers finish) and an **outer board**. Points are numbered 1 to 24 from each player's own point of view: your 1-point is the deepest point of your home board, and your 24-point is the far end, in your opponent's home board.

A raised strip down the middle is called **the bar**. Checkers that are hit wait there.

## Setup

Each player has 15 checkers, placed like this (counting from your own side):

| Point | Checkers |
| --- | --- |
| 24-point (opponent's home board) | 2 |
| 13-point (opponent's outer board) | 5 |
| 8-point (your outer board) | 3 |
| 6-point (your home board) | 5 |

The two players move in opposite directions, each from their 24-point toward their 1-point.

## Moving

On your turn, roll two dice. Each die is a separate move: with a 5 and a 2, you can move one checker five points and another two points, or one checker five and then two (or two and then five).

- A checker may land on an **empty point**, a point with your own checkers, or a point with **a single** enemy checker.
- It may **not** land on a point with two or more enemy checkers; that point is blocked.
- With **doubles**, you play the number four times.
- You must use both dice if you legally can. If only one can be played, play that one; if either one but not both can be played, you must play the higher.

## Hitting and the bar

A single checker on a point is a **blot**. If your opponent lands on it, it is **hit** and goes to the bar. Before you may move anything else, you must bring it back in by **entering** it into your opponent's home board: a roll of 3 lets you enter on their 3-point (your 22-point), and so on, as long as that point is not blocked.

If every entry point you rolled is blocked, you lose your whole turn. This is why building a wall of blocked points in your home board is so strong.

## Bearing off

Once **all 15** of your checkers are in your home board, you can start **bearing off** (removing them). A roll of 4 removes a checker from your 4-point. If there is no checker on that point, you must move a checker from a higher point if you can; if there are no checkers on higher points, you may bear off from the highest occupied point.

If one of your checkers is hit while you are bearing off, it must come all the way around again before you can continue.

## Winning and scoring

The first player to bear off all 15 checkers wins.

| Result | When | Value |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Single game | The loser has borne off at least one checker | 1 |
| Gammon | The loser has not borne off any checker | 2 |
| Backgammon | The loser has borne off none and still has a checker on the bar or in the winner's home board | 3 |

Many players also use a **doubling cube** to raise the stakes during a game, but beginners can safely ignore it.

## The opening roll

To start, each player commonly rolls one die; the higher roll goes first and plays both numbers as its first move. If both dice match, roll again.

## A first strategy

You do not need deep theory to play well. These five ideas cover most decisions:

1. **Make points.** Two checkers on a point are safe and block your opponent. Points in your home board and on your 7-point (the bar point) are the most valuable.
2. **Limit blots.** A single checker can be hit. Leave one only when it is far from enemy checkers or when it helps you build a point next turn.
3. **Hit when it helps.** Sending an enemy checker back costs your opponent many moves, especially if your home board is strong.
4. **Escape your back checkers.** The two checkers on your 24-point have the longest way home. Move them out early, before your opponent builds a wall.
5. **Race when ahead.** If your checkers have passed all of your opponent's, stop fighting and run for home.

Common good opening moves include 3-1 (make your 5-point: 8/5 and 6/5) and 6-1 (make your bar point: 13/7 and 8/7).
