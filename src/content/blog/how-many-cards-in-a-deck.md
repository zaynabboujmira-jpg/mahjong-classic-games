The standard deck of playing cards is one of the most familiar objects in the world, but quick questions about it come up all the time: how many hearts are there, how many face cards, how many cards with the jokers? This guide answers all of them in one place, with the numbers for the card games you can play on this site.

## The short answer: 52 cards

A standard deck (also called a pack) has **52 cards**, divided into **4 suits of 13 cards** each:

| Suit | Symbol | Colour | Cards |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Spades | ♠ | Black | 13 |
| Hearts | ♥ | Red | 13 |
| Diamonds | ♦ | Red | 13 |
| Clubs | ♣ | Black | 13 |

Each suit has the same 13 ranks: **ace, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, jack, queen and king**.

The **face cards** (also called court cards) are the jacks, queens and kings, so there are three per suit and twelve in all. Aces are not face cards. In most games they rank highest, above the king, but in some, such as Cribbage and many solitaire games, the ace is low and counts as 1.

## Red and black

Hearts and diamonds are printed in red, spades and clubs in black. With two suits of each colour, a deck has **26 red cards and 26 black cards**. Colour matters in many solitaire games: in Klondike and FreeCell you build columns down in alternating colours, a red 6 on a black 7, then a black 5 on the red 6.

## Jokers and extra cards

Most decks come with **two jokers**, bringing the total to **54**. Jokers have no suit or rank of their own. Most classic games leave them in the box. A few games use them as wild cards, and some house versions of Spades add them as the two highest trumps.

Many packs also include one or two extra cards with the rules of a game or the maker's details. They are not part of the deck.

## Why 52 cards?

Nobody designed the deck around a single number. People often point out that 52 matches the weeks in a year, 4 suits match the seasons and 13 ranks match the lunar months, but these are coincidences noticed later, not the reason. The 52-card pack is simply the French deck of four suits with ten number cards and three court cards each, which became the standard in Britain and then most of the world.

## Some quick probabilities

Because every card appears exactly once, the chances are easy to work out:

- Drawing a **heart** from a full deck: 13 in 52, or **1 in 4**.
- Drawing an **ace**: 4 in 52, or **1 in 13**.
- Drawing a **face card**: 12 in 52, or **about 23%**.
- Drawing a **specific card**, such as the queen of spades: **1 in 52**.

The number of different ways to order a deck is **52 factorial**, about 8 × 10⁶⁷. Shuffle a deck properly and the order in your hands has almost certainly never existed before.

## Where the suits come from

The four suits used in English-speaking countries, spades, hearts, diamonds and clubs, are the French suits, which appeared in France in the late 15th century. Cards themselves came to Europe in the 1300s. Other traditions use different suits: Spanish and Italian decks have cups, coins, swords and clubs, and German decks have hearts, bells, leaves and acorns. Many Spanish and Italian decks have 40 or 48 cards rather than 52.

## How many cards each game uses

| Game | Cards | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [Hearts](/cards/hearts/) | 52 | 13 hearts worth 1 point each, plus 13 for the queen of spades |
| [Spades](/cards/spades/) | 52 | All 13 spades are trumps |
| [Cribbage](/cards/cribbage/) | 52 | Six cards each for two players, plus a starter |
| [Klondike](/solitaire/klondike/) | 52 | 28 dealt to the tableau, 24 in the stock |
| [FreeCell](/solitaire/freecell/) | 52 | All dealt face up into eight columns |
| [Pyramid](/solitaire/pyramid/) | 52 | 28 in the pyramid, 24 in the stock |
| [Spider](/solitaire/spider/) | 104 | Two full decks |

In **Hearts**, the 13 hearts and the queen of spades add up to **26 penalty points** each hand, which is why taking all of them, shooting the moon, is such a big swing. In **Spades**, knowing there are exactly 13 spades lets you count how many trumps are still out. [Hearts Rules and Shooting the Moon](/blog/hearts-rules-and-shooting-the-moon/) and [How to Play Spades](/blog/how-to-play-spades-rules/) explain both games.

## Counting cards in play

Knowing the makeup of the deck is the first step to counting cards in any game. In a trick-taking game, keep track of how many cards of each suit have been played: once 10 hearts have gone, only 3 remain, and you can work out who might still hold them. In solitaire, remembering which cards are buried in the stock tells you which moves can wait. All the card games on this site deal from a standard deck, so these numbers hold in every one of them.
