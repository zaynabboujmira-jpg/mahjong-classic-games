Mahjong Solitaire looks like a game of spotting pairs, but spotting is the easy part. The tiles you take early decide which tiles you can reach late, and a board that looks open with forty tiles left can already be lost. The nine tactics below are what separate a lucky win from a reliable one.

## How a tile becomes free

A tile can be matched only when it is **free**: nothing lies on top of it, and its left or right side is open. Tiles above or below it on the same layer do not matter. This one rule explains every tactic in this guide. Long rows are slow to open because only their two ends are free. Tall stacks are slow because only the top tile is free. Everything you do is about opening those two kinds of blockage in the right order.

## 1. Look before you match

Free pairs are everywhere at the start, and the temptation is to take the first one you see. Instead, before each match, ask one question: **what does this pair uncover?** A pair that frees two new tiles is worth far more than a pair that frees nothing. If a match uncovers nothing, it can almost always wait.

## 2. Open the long rows from their ends

The longest horizontal rows hold the most tiles that cannot move. Each match at the end of a row frees the next tile along, so working inwards from both ends of the longest rows steadily turns blocked tiles into free ones. On the Turtle layout, the wide middle rows and the single tiles that cap their ends are the main targets.

## 3. Dig into the tall stacks early

The centre of most layouts is a pyramid of four or five layers. Every tile under the top one is locked until the layers above are gone, and they often hide the partners you need later. Spending your early matches on the top of the stack, rather than on easy pairs at the edges, keeps the tallest pile from becoming the reason you lose.

## 4. Count the four copies

Every tile face appears exactly four times (the seasons and flowers are the exception: any season matches any season, and any flower matches any flower). Counting helps in three situations:

- **Two copies free, two buried:** match the free pair if it opens something; the buried pair will still be a pair later.
- **Three copies free:** choose the two whose removal frees the most tiles, and keep the third for its buried partner.
- **All four free:** take both pairs straight away. There is no reason to wait.

## 5. Watch for tiles that block their own twin

The classic dead end is a tile sitting on top of, or beside, the only remaining copy of itself. Neither can be freed while the other is still there. When you see two identical tiles stacked together, make freeing them a priority while other copies of that face are still on the board to pair with.

## 6. Do not clear an edge just because you can

Tiles on the outside edges are usually free from the start and stay free. Clearing them early wastes matches that could have opened the middle. Treat edge pairs as a reserve: they are the moves you play when nothing better is available.

## 7. Plan two moves ahead with pairs that share a tile

Sometimes two different pairs compete for one tile. For example, three free copies of the same character sit in different places. Picture the board after each choice: which one leaves a new free pair behind? Thinking two matches ahead is enough to avoid most self-made traps.

## 8. Use hints and undo as teachers

A hint shows a free pair, not the best pair, so it is useful when you cannot see any move but not as a strategy. Undo is more valuable. When a game ends badly, step back a few moves and look at which match blocked you. You will often find the mistake was ten or twenty matches earlier. That lesson is what makes the next game easier.

## 9. Pick the layout to match your goal

Different layouts reward different habits:

| Layout | Tiles | What to focus on |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Turtle | 144 | The long middle rows and the single capping tiles at each end |
| Dragon | 140 | The wide wings, which block many tiles behind them |
| Pyramid | 120 | Peeling the four-layer central stack from the top down |
| Fortress | 144 | The long, low walls: work them from both ends |
| Butterfly | 128 | Balancing the two wings so neither side is left locked |
| Fish | 144 | The long body rows, opened from head and tail |

If you are learning, start on Turtle: it is the classic shape and teaches both row and stack tactics.

## A short routine for every game

1. Scan the board for the tallest stack and the longest rows.
2. Take matches that open those first, choosing pairs that free new tiles.
3. Keep edge pairs and matches that free nothing in reserve.
4. Count copies whenever three or four of a face are visible.
5. When stuck, undo a few moves before you reach for the shuffle.

Follow it for a few games and you will notice fewer endings with six or eight tiles left. Because every deal on this site can be solved, each loss is a puzzle you can replay until you find the order that works.
