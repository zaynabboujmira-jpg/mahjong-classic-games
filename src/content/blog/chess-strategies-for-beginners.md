Learning the moves takes an afternoon. Winning games takes a few habits that strong players use without thinking. These ten chess strategies for beginners are the ones that make the biggest difference, in roughly the order you will need them. None of them requires memorising opening lines.

## 1. Check, capture, threat: before every move

The single most useful habit in chess is a quick safety check before you move. Ask three questions:

1. **What did my opponent's last move threaten?** Look for checks, captures and attacks on your pieces.
2. **Does my move leave something undefended?** A piece that moves may stop guarding another.
3. **Can I give check, capture or make a threat?** Look at forcing moves first, for both sides.

This takes ten seconds and prevents most lost games at beginner level.

## 2. Count material

Every piece has a rough value. Use it to judge trades:

| Piece | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Pawn | 1 |
| Knight | 3 |
| Bishop | 3 |
| Rook | 5 |
| Queen | 9 |

Giving a knight for a rook wins two points. Giving a bishop for three pawns is roughly equal. When you are **ahead in material, trade pieces** (not pawns): the fewer pieces left, the more your extra one counts.

## 3. Develop, castle and take the centre

In the opening, bring out your knights and bishops, put a pawn in the centre and castle early. A king left in the middle while the centre opens is the most common way beginners lose quickly. The [chess openings guide for beginners](/blog/chess-opening-principles-for-beginners/) covers this in detail, with five simple openings to try.

## 4. Do not leave pieces loose

A loose piece is one that nobody defends. It is the first target for every tactic. When you move a piece, make sure it is either defended or safe from attack. When your opponent has a loose piece, look for a way to attack it twice.

## 5. Learn the four basic tactics

Most games between beginners are won by one of these:

- **Fork:** one piece attacks two at once. Knights are the best forkers, because their moves are easy to overlook.
- **Pin:** a piece cannot move without exposing a more valuable piece behind it. A bishop pinning a knight to the king is the classic example.
- **Skewer:** the reverse of a pin. A valuable piece is attacked, moves away, and the piece behind it is taken.
- **Discovered attack:** moving one piece uncovers an attack by another. If the moving piece also attacks something, your opponent cannot answer both.

Look for these in every position, for yourself and against yourself. [Chess Tactics for Beginners](/blog/chess-tactics-for-beginners/) explains each one with examples, plus the checkmate patterns worth knowing.

## 6. Make a simple plan

When there is nothing to capture and no threat to answer, beginners often make random moves. Use one of these plans instead:

- **Improve your worst piece.** A knight on the edge or a bishop blocked by its own pawns is doing nothing; find it a better square.
- **Put rooks on open files.** A file with no pawns on it is a highway for a rook. Two rooks on the same file are even stronger.
- **Attack where you are stronger.** If more of your pieces point at one side of the board, that is where your attack belongs.

## 7. Look after your pawns

Pawns cannot move backwards, so every pawn move is permanent. A few rules of thumb:

- Avoid moving the pawns in front of your castled king without a reason; they are its shelter.
- **Doubled pawns** (two on one file) and **isolated pawns** (no friendly pawn on either neighbouring file) are weaker than they look.
- A **passed pawn**, with no enemy pawn in front of it or on the neighbouring files, is a future queen. Push it when it is safe.

## 8. Know when to trade

Trades are not neutral. Trade when you are ahead in material, when you are under attack and want to reduce the attackers, or when your opponent's piece is much better than yours. Avoid trades when you are behind, or when your opponent is cramped and short of good squares.

## 9. Win the endgame

With most pieces gone, the rules change:

- **Activate your king.** In the endgame the king is a strong piece. March it towards the centre and the pawns.
- **Push passed pawns** and stop your opponent's.
- **Learn the basic checkmates:** king and queen against king, and king and rook against king. Both can be forced every time.
- **Watch for stalemate.** If your opponent has no legal move and is not in check, the game is a draw. Always leave the enemy king a square until you deliver mate.

The rules for stalemate and the other draws are in [Chess Special Moves](/blog/chess-special-moves-castling-en-passant/).

## 10. Learn from every loss

After a lost game, replay it and find the move where things went wrong. It is usually a missed threat or a loose piece, which brings you back to tip 1. Playing slower and checking more is the fastest way to improve.

## A practice plan

Play against the [chess game on this site](/board/chess/), which has three computer levels:

1. Start on **Beginner** and practise the safety check (tip 1) until you stop losing pieces for nothing.
2. Move up to **Club**, which plays solid, sensible chess, and look for the tactics in tip 5.
3. Try **Tactician** for a real challenge. It looks up to six moves ahead and punishes loose pieces quickly.

If you are new to the game, set up the pieces with the [chess board setup guide](/blog/how-to-set-up-a-chess-board/) first. Use undo to replay any position you got wrong, and play both White and Black: you will learn the openings from both sides.
