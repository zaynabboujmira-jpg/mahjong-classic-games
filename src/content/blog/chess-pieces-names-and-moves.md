Every game of chess uses the same six kinds of pieces, and each one moves in its own way. Once you know the names and the moves, you can play a full game. This guide goes through the pieces one by one, from the king to the humble pawn, with what each is worth and the special moves that surprise most beginners.

## The six chess pieces at a glance

| Piece | Letter | How many | How it moves | Value |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| King | K | 1 | One square in any direction | Priceless |
| Queen | Q | 1 | Any distance in a straight line or diagonal | 9 |
| Rook | R | 2 | Any distance along a rank or file | 5 |
| Bishop | B | 2 | Any distance diagonally | 3 |
| Knight | N | 2 | An L-shape: two squares, then one to the side | 3 |
| Pawn | none | 8 | One square forward; captures diagonally | 1 |

Pieces capture by moving onto a square held by an enemy piece, which is then removed from the board. Apart from the pawn, every piece captures the same way it moves. No piece can capture one of its own side.

## The king

The king moves **one square in any direction**: forward, back, sideways or diagonally. It is not a strong attacker, but it is the reason the game exists. When a king is attacked it is in **check**, and the player must get out of check at once. If there is no way out, that is **checkmate**, and the game is over.

A king may never move onto a square where it would be in check, so the two kings can never stand side by side.

## The queen

The queen is the most powerful piece. It moves **any number of squares in a straight line**: along a rank, along a file or along a diagonal, as long as nothing blocks the way. In the middle of an empty board it can reach 27 squares. Because it is worth so much, beginners should avoid bringing it out too early, where it can be chased around by cheaper pieces.

## The rook

The rook (often called a castle) moves **any number of squares along a rank or a file**, but never diagonally. Rooks start in the corners and are slow to join the game, but they become very strong on open files with no pawns in the way, and when two rooks work together on the same rank or file.

## The bishop

The bishop moves **any number of squares diagonally**. That means a bishop stays on the same colour all game: each side has a light-squared bishop and a dark-squared bishop. Bishops are at their best on long, open diagonals, and the pair of bishops working together is a real advantage in the endgame.

## The knight

The knight moves in an **L-shape**: two squares in one direction, then one square at a right angle. It always lands on a square of the other colour from where it started. The knight is the **only piece that jumps over others**, so it is never blocked, which makes it useful in crowded positions and dangerous for forks, where one knight attacks two pieces at once.

Its letter in notation is **N**, because K already belongs to the king.

## The pawn

Pawns are the foot soldiers. A pawn moves **one square straight forward**, never backwards. On its very first move it may move **two squares** forward instead. Unlike every other piece, a pawn **captures diagonally**: one square forward to the left or right. A pawn that has an enemy piece directly in front of it is stuck.

When a pawn reaches the far side of the board it **promotes**: it is replaced by a queen, rook, bishop or knight of the same colour. Almost everyone chooses a queen, so a single pawn can become the strongest piece on the board.

## Special moves

Three moves break the normal patterns:

- **Castling:** the king moves two squares towards a rook, and that rook jumps to the square on the king's other side. It is allowed only if neither piece has moved, the squares between them are empty, and the king is not in check and does not pass through or land on an attacked square.
- **En passant:** if a pawn moves two squares and lands beside an enemy pawn, the enemy pawn may capture it as if it had moved only one square. This must be done immediately, on the next move.
- **Promotion:** described above, when a pawn reaches the last rank.

All three are explained step by step in [Chess Special Moves: Castling, En Passant and Promotion](/blog/chess-special-moves-castling-en-passant/).

## What the points mean

The values in the table are a guide for trades, not a rule of the game. Giving up a knight (3) to win a rook (5) usually leaves you ahead, and a bishop and a knight together are worth about the same as a rook and a pawn. Position matters too: a knight in the centre is worth more than a knight stuck on the edge. Count the material when you are deciding whether to capture, and you will stop giving pieces away for nothing.

## Where the pieces start

At the start, rooks go in the corners, then knights, then bishops, with the queen on her own colour and the king beside her, and a full row of pawns in front. [How to Set Up a Chess Board](/blog/how-to-set-up-a-chess-board/) has a checklist so you get it right every time.
