Most of chess is simple to learn: each piece moves in its own way, and you win by checkmating the king. Then a few rules surprise almost every beginner. "You can't castle there." "That pawn just took mine sideways?" "Why is it a draw, I have a queen?" This guide explains the three special moves and the draw rules that catch people out.

## Castling

Castling is the only move in which two pieces move at once. It gets your king to safety and brings a rook toward the centre.

### How to castle

Move the **king two squares** toward one of your rooks. The **rook then jumps** to the square the king just crossed.

| Side | King moves | Rook moves |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Kingside (short), White | e1 to g1 | h1 to f1 |
| Queenside (long), White | e1 to c1 | a1 to d1 |
| Kingside (short), Black | e8 to g8 | h8 to f8 |
| Queenside (long), Black | e8 to c8 | a8 to d8 |

On a screen, you usually castle by moving the king two squares; the rook follows automatically.

### When castling is not allowed

You may not castle if:

1. the **king has moved** earlier in the game, even if it moved back;
2. **that rook has moved** earlier in the game;
3. any square **between** the king and the rook is occupied;
4. your king is **in check** right now;
5. the king would **pass through** a square attacked by an enemy piece;
6. the king would **land in check**.

The rook is allowed to be attacked, and on the queenside it may pass through an attacked square (b1 or b8); only the king's path matters.

## En passant

En passant (French for "in passing") is a pawn capture that only exists right after a pawn's two-square first move.

### How it works

A pawn on its starting square may move two squares forward. If that move lands it **directly beside an enemy pawn** (on the same rank), the enemy pawn may capture it **as if it had moved only one square**: the capturing pawn moves diagonally onto the square that was skipped, and the pawn that moved two squares is removed.

Example: a white pawn stands on e5. Black plays d7 to d5, landing beside it. White may now play e5 takes d6 en passant; the black pawn on d5 is removed.

### The one-move rule

The capture must be made **on the very next move**. If you play anything else, the chance is gone for good. En passant is only possible for pawns, only against a pawn that has just made a two-square move, and only from the fifth rank (for White) or fourth rank (for Black).

## Pawn promotion

When a pawn reaches the **far rank** (the 8th for White, the 1st for Black), it must be **promoted**: replaced by a queen, rook, bishop or knight of the same colour.

- You may choose any of the four, **regardless of what is already on the board**. Two or even three queens are legal.
- You cannot keep it as a pawn or turn it into a king.
- Promoting to something other than a queen is called **underpromotion**. It is occasionally the only winning move, for example a knight promotion that gives check, or a rook to avoid stalemate.

## Check, checkmate and stalemate

- **Check:** your king is attacked. You must get out of check on your move, by moving the king, blocking or capturing the attacker.
- **Checkmate:** your king is in check and nothing gets it out. The game is over and the attacker wins.
- **Stalemate:** it is your move, your king is **not** in check, but you have **no legal move**. The game is a **draw**.

Stalemate is the classic beginner trap: with a big material advantage, you take away every square from the enemy king without giving check, and half a point vanishes. When you are winning, always check that your opponent still has a move.

## Other draws

| Draw | When it happens |
| --- | --- |
| Threefold repetition | The same position occurs three times with the same player to move |
| Fifty-move rule | Fifty moves by each side with no capture and no pawn move |
| Insufficient material | Neither side can possibly checkmate, for example king against king, or king and bishop against king |
| Agreement | Both players agree to a draw |

## Five rule myths

- **"You can't castle after being checked."** You can, as long as the king and that rook have not moved and you are not in check right now.
- **"A pawn can only promote to a captured piece."** No: any queen, rook, bishop or knight, whatever is still on the board.
- **"The king can't move next to the enemy king in check."** Kings can never stand next to each other at all, because each would be in check.
- **"Running out of time with a bare king loses."** In timed games, if your opponent has no way to checkmate, running out of time is usually a draw.
- **"Touching a piece is fine in casual play."** In tournaments, a piece you deliberately touch must be moved if it has a legal move.
