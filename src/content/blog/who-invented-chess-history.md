Chess is so old that nobody can name its inventor. Instead, it grew and changed over about 1,500 years, picking up new pieces, new moves and new names as it travelled from India to the rest of the world. This guide follows that journey, from the earliest board games to world championships and computers.

## Chaturanga: the Indian ancestor

Most historians trace chess back to **chaturanga**, played in northern India by around the 6th century, during or soon after the Gupta period. Its name means "four divisions", after the four branches of an ancient Indian army: infantry, cavalry, elephants and chariots. Those became the pawns, knights, bishops and rooks of today.

Chaturanga was played on an 8×8 board, and many of its pieces already moved much like ours. The rook moved in straight lines and the knight in its L-shape. The king had a counsellor beside him instead of a queen, and both the counsellor and the elephant were weak, short-range pieces.

## Shatranj in Persia and the Arab world

By the 7th century the game had reached **Persia**, where it was called **chatrang** and then **shatranj**. Many chess words come from Persian: "checkmate" comes from *shah mat*, often translated as "the king is helpless", and "rook" comes from *rukh*.

After the Arab conquest of Persia, shatranj spread across the Islamic world. Players there studied it seriously: they wrote books of openings and endgame problems, ranked the strongest players, and recorded games. Shatranj was slow by modern standards, because the counsellor (*firzan*) and the elephant (*alfil*) could only move one or two squares.

## Chess reaches Europe

Shatranj reached **Europe** through Muslim Spain, Sicily and trade routes, and was known across much of the continent by around the **10th and 11th centuries**. European players changed the pieces to fit their own world: the counsellor became the **queen**, the elephant became the **bishop** (or the fool, in French), and the chariot kept its Persian name as the **rook**.

Chess became part of courtly life. It appears in medieval poems and sermons, and by the 13th century there were European books of chess problems.

## The birth of modern chess

The biggest change came in **southern Europe in the late 15th century**, probably first in Spain or Italy. The queen gained the power to move any distance in any direction, and the bishop gained its long diagonal move. The game suddenly became much faster and more aggressive, and the new rules spread across Europe within a few decades. One of the earliest printed chess books, by Luis Ramírez de Lucena, appeared in Spain around 1497.

Smaller rules settled over the next centuries. The pawn's two-square first move led to **en passant**, and **castling** took its modern form in the 16th and 17th centuries. [Chess Special Moves](/blog/chess-special-moves-castling-en-passant/) explains how both work today.

## Clubs, champions and the modern game

In the 18th and 19th centuries chess became a game of clubs and coffee houses. The first international tournament was held in **London in 1851**. The **Staunton** design of pieces, the shape you see in most sets today, was introduced in **1849**.

The first official **World Championship** match was played in **1886**, won by Wilhelm Steinitz. The international chess federation, **FIDE**, was founded in Paris in **1924**, and it still organises the World Championship and the Chess Olympiad.

## Computers and chess

Chess has long been a test for computers. In **1997**, the IBM machine **Deep Blue** beat world champion Garry Kasparov in a six-game match, the first time a computer had beaten a reigning champion in a match under standard time controls. Today, chess engines on a phone play far better than any human, and players use them to study openings and check their games. Online play has made chess more popular than ever.

## Chess cousins around the world

Chaturanga has other descendants besides chess. **Xiangqi** (Chinese chess) is played on the points of a 9×10 board with a river across the middle. **Shogi** (Japanese chess) lets players drop captured pieces back onto the board as their own. **Makruk** in Thailand stays close to the old Indian game. All share chess's central idea: protect your king and trap your opponent's.

## The legend of the inventor

A famous story says a wise man presented chess to a king, who offered any reward. The inventor asked for one grain of wheat on the first square of the board, two on the second, four on the third, doubling each time. The king agreed, only to discover that the total, more than 18 quintillion grains, was more wheat than the world could grow. It is a legend, but a good lesson in how fast doubling grows.
